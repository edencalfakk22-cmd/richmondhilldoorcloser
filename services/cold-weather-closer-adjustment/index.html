Almost every closer failure has a date attached to it, and the date is usually about six weeks before anyone called. A door that latches in September is closing a little more slowly by late October, needs a nudge by November, and fails on the first genuinely cold night. Hydraulic fluid thickens as it cools, so the same valve setting delivers a slower door, and a slower door arrives at the strike without the momentum to latch.

A round is not complicated. We walk the openings, check each unit for fluid loss and for arm or bracket damage, set sweep speed, latch speed and backcheck where the unit allows, release every rated door from a full open position to confirm it latches unaided, and measure opening force where the route has to stay barrier-free. That last check matters more than it sounds: the Building Code limits opening force on a door in a barrier-free path of travel, and winding a spring up to beat a draft is the most common way that limit gets broken by somebody trying to help.

Portfolios get the most out of this. A condo tower, a school, a plaza strip or a professional building has enough openings that one scheduled autumn pass costs less than the emergency visits it prevents — and you finish with a written list of what was adjusted and what is heading for replacement, which is what a reserve budget needs in order to plan at all.

Closing-Speed Adjustment & Preventive Maintenance — your questions

What is the difference between sweep, latch and backcheck?

Sweep controls the main part of the closing swing. Latch speed controls the last few inches, where the bolt actually has to engage. Backcheck slows the door near the end of the opening swing so wind cannot fling it into the wall. They are separate valves solving separate complaints, which is why turning the closer down rarely fixes what somebody is actually annoyed by.

Is a maintenance round worth it on a building where nothing is broken?

That is the best time to do one. Closers fail gradually — a door closing a little slower each week for two months, then failing to latch on the first cold night. Catching that in October costs an adjustment; catching it in January costs an emergency visit and usually a unit. You also get a written list of what is heading for replacement, which is what a reserve budget needs.

Do the doors up in Oak Ridges really need different settings?

In practice, yes. Oak Ridges sits high on the moraine and is noticeably more exposed than the rest of the town, so a door up there meets more wind and colder air than an identical door in Crosby or Harding. Setting every closer in a portfolio to one number is how the exposed doors end up being the ones that fail.
