    Fire doors in Richmond Hill condo and apartment common areas

    Ontario's Fire Code is blunt about this: closures in a fire separation have to be maintained so they close and latch properly, and they must never be obstructed, blocked or wedged open. In practice that means the stairwell door, the garbage-chute room door, the parking-garage vestibule and every suite entrance in a Richmond Hill condo or rental building has to swing shut and latch by itself, from any open position, with nobody helping it. The Code also expects doors in a fire separation to be inspected monthly, which is exactly the kind of walk-through that turns up the same three findings in building after building — a closer that was disconnected years ago, a door that closes but rests against the strike without latching, and a wedge under a corridor door that somebody left there in the spring. Richmond Hill Fire and Emergency Services runs inspections through its Fire Prevention Division, so property managers here get asked to show that this work is being done, not just that the doors look fine.

  • 🔧

    Office entrances in Beaver Creek and the Highway 7 corridor

    Beaver Creek Business Park sits in the corner of Richmond Hill closest to the 404 and 407, and the low-rise office condominiums along East and West Beaver Creek Road are full of the openings that eat closers: aluminum-and-glass entry doors on exposed corners, vestibules with an inner and outer door fighting each other, and demising doors into shared corridors that are also fire separations. Add the office and medical buildings along Highway 7 East and the towers going up around Richmond Hill Centre and Langstaff GO, and you have a lot of doors on a lot of maintenance contracts. Multi-tenant buildings are where a single badly set closer causes the most trouble — one entrance that slams gets reported by five tenants, and one that never latches leaves a shared corridor open to a stairwell.

    Hydraulic closers stiffening in the York Region cold

    Hydraulic fluid thickens as it gets colder, which is why the same closer that felt perfect in August turns slow and heavy at minus eighteen. The door drifts, stalls a few centimetres short of the strike and never latches, or the entrance simply hangs open with the heat pouring out and the vestibule floor icing over. Richmond Hill sits north of Toronto and further from the lake, so it reliably runs colder than the downtown core on the same night — closers set once and never revisited spend a good part of the year out of spec here. Sweep speed, latch speed and backcheck are separate adjustments, and the fix is setting them for the season, not cranking the spring up until nobody can pull the door open.

  • 🔧

    Freeze-thaw, brine and grit at street-level entrances

    York Region crosses the freezing mark dozens of times over a winter, and every one of those swings drags meltwater, road salt and grit into a doorway. On plaza and office entrances along Yonge, Highway 7, Major Mackenzie and Elgin Mills that mixture is brutal on floor-spring cases set into the slab at grade, on closer arms and shoe brackets, and on the pivots the door hangs from. When a closer suddenly feels rough, weeps oil around a cover plate, or a door that latched in November stops latching in March, the damage is usually in the arm, the pivot or the floor spring underneath — not in the valves everybody reaches for first.

  • 🔧

    Humid summers, swollen doors and barrier-free opening force

    The other half of the Ontario year does the opposite. In July and August humidity pushes into wood and hollow-metal doors, gaskets and smoke seals get tacky, and a door that latched cleanly in the cold starts binding on the frame. The usual field fix is to wind the closer spring up until the door slams home, which works right until December — when the same door becomes one an older resident, a parent with a stroller or somebody using a mobility device cannot pull open, and a barrier-free entrance quietly stops meeting the opening-force limit the Building Code sets for it. We solve the binding at the door and frame instead, and leave the closer set where it belongs.

Richmond Hill FAQs

Your questions, answered

What does the Ontario Fire Code actually require of our door closers?

In plain terms, closures in a fire separation have to be maintained in operating condition — the door closes and latches on its own, from any open position, with nobody helping it — and they must not be obstructed, blocked or wedged open. The Code specifically calls for the adjustments and repairs to door hardware needed to ensure proper closing and latching, and for inoperative hold-open devices to be repaired or replaced. A door that swings shut but rests against the strike does not meet that standard.

How often do fire doors need to be inspected in a Richmond Hill building?

The Fire Code expects doors in a fire separation to be checked monthly, with the surrounding area kept clear of anything that would interfere with the door operating freely. Most property managers here have staff do the monthly walk and bring us in for a professional round twice a year, so what gets found actually gets corrected and documented.

Why do our closers get so much worse every winter?

Hydraulic fluid thickens as it cools, so the closer that felt right in August moves slowly at minus eighteen and often stops short of the latch. Richmond Hill runs colder than the lakeshore on the same night, and in taller buildings winter stack pressure adds to it by pushing and pulling on stairwell doors. Both are fixed with seasonal adjustment, not with a heavier spring nobody can pull against.

Can a leaking door closer be topped up instead of replaced?

No. Closers are sealed hydraulic units with no fill port, so once the fluid is lost the body has to be replaced. Leaking closers either slam hard enough to damage the door and frame or stall short of the latch, which on a fire door is a compliance problem as well as an annoyance.

Do you work on whole buildings, or only single doors?

Both, but building-wide is where we do our best work. Condo and apartment towers, schools, plazas and office floors get far more out of one scheduled pass over every opening than out of a string of separate call-outs, and you get a written list of what was adjusted and what still needs parts.

Are you licensed?

Ontario does not issue a provincial trade licence for door hardware work, so nobody in this trade legitimately holds one and we will never show you a number that does not exist. What we can show you is real: liability insurance, WSIB coverage, and a written workmanship guarantee on everything we install and repair.
